# Why this exists

The reasoning is in **Measure, Don't Look** (`r2-blueprint/docs/measure-dont-look.html`).
This engine is that document's finding 10 made runnable, and its finding 14
taken seriously: the kit is the deliverable.

## The findings this is built on

**01 — Vision review does not converge. Measurement does.** Every defect that
survived more than one round of review survived because it was judged by looking
at a picture. The productive loop is *render → extract → calibrate → diff in
millimetres → edit*. This engine removes the render from that loop entirely: a
silhouette taken from a screenshot is a measurement of the renderer as much as
of the model, and moves with the camera, the lens and the resolution. A section
sliced from the mesh is the curve the model is actually made of.

**03 — Calibrate on landmarks that cannot move. Every measurement harness needs
a self-test.** `npm run selftest` drives every instrument against a fixture
whose answer is known by construction, then refuses to certify a real model
until its axle-to-axle distance comes back as the published wheelbase.

**12 — A measurement is only as good as the argument that the quantity moves.**
Every instrument here has a positive *and* a negative control. A ruler that
reports a wheelbase for a model with no wheels is not measuring a wheelbase.

**14 — An instrument that does not survive the session is not phase 0.** This is
committed, versioned and one command per question.

## What this build taught, on top of those

**A naive envelope measures the mirrors.** The first bounding box over the R2
returned 2.151 m wide against a 1.905 m specification. Both numbers are correct:
2.151 is `widthMirrors`. A dimension without its landmarks is not a measurement,
it is a coincidence — so every number this engine reports carries the parts it
was taken from, and `dims` reports width over body and over mirrors separately
because "how wide is it" has two honest answers.

**Triangle centroids are a proxy for the surface, and a bad one.** The first
symmetry check mirrored triangle centroids across the centreline and reported
**52% symmetric** for a car that is symmetric by construction. It was measuring
the *tessellation*: the two pillar surrounds are generated by walking the
section in opposite directions, so the surfaces match and the triangles do not.
Comparing sections instead gives **98.6% within 2 mm**, and localises the
remainder to the mirrored brake calipers and the charge port — which are
asymmetric on purpose. The wrong instrument was deleted rather than kept
alongside the right one.

**Centre-to-centre is not clearance.** The digital twin's own `measure` tool
subtracts bounding-box centres, so two interpenetrating parts and two parts
200 mm apart return the same number. Worse, a part assembled from pieces
scattered across the vehicle — the brakes live inside the four wheel hubs — has
a centre at the middle of the car and a distance to everything of about 1.7 m.
`clearance` reports nearest surfaces, the exact axis gaps, and centre-to-centre
labelled as such.

**The self-test's first two failures were wrong expectations, not wrong
instruments.** Two blocks with centres 600 mm apart and 400 mm deep have a
100 mm gap only if the centres are 650 mm apart; at 600 the gap is 200 mm, which
is what the instrument said. The comment was wrong. Left in the source as a
reminder that the number under suspicion has been the correct one twice running.

## What the reference side taught

**Do not re-implement a working instrument to own it.** `measure_ortho.py`
already does the hard image work — thresholding, connected components, reading
the drawing's own dimension extension lines, warping the overhangs. Rewriting
that in JavaScript would have produced a second thing to keep in step, which is
the drift problem this project has already paid for once. The engine consumes
its output and adds the half nobody had built.

**A reference must be refused, not just annotated.** Loading the side drawing
reproduces the documented finding automatically: height agrees to 0.06%, length
is 4.36% short. The loader reports which landmark it is trusting and refuses a
source where none agrees. A knot list fitted to a drawing inherits that
drawing's calibration, so the calibration travels with it.

**Fit with the consumer's own interpolator.** The knot fitter imports `interp`
from the model's `geom.js`. A list fitted against a different curve than the
model evaluates reproduces approximately, and approximately is what this engine
exists to replace.

**One number cannot judge an outline.** The first model-to-drawing comparison
reported a 71 mm mean for the lower profile, which is meaningless: at the axles
that curve is the tyre contact patch and both sources read 0.000, while between
them it is whatever hangs lowest. Reported by region it becomes 15 mm at the
wheels and 98 mm between them — and the second number is a real question about
whether the visible line at mid-wheelbase is the rocker or the pack.

**I excluded the wheels first, and was wrong.** The reference bottom reads
0.000 at both axles, which is the tyre touching the ground, so the drawing's
lower outline includes the wheels. Measured rather than assumed, after the
first answer looked wrong — the same discipline that caught the two wrong
expectations in the self-test.

## What closing the loop taught

**The two frames were not the same, and it was being measured as shape error.**
The extractor centres the vehicle on its length; the model centres it on its
wheelbase. The overhangs differ by 102 mm, so the frames agree at the axles and
disagree by about 51 mm at each end. Measured from the drawing's own tyre
contact patches: its wheelbase centre sits 9.3 mm forward of the model's and its
wheelbase is 10.5 mm short. Unaligned, that offset was appearing as shape error
in the overhang regions.

**And which frame is right is not the tool's call.** Aligning on the axles
improves the wheels and both overhangs and makes the roof mid-section worse,
0.58 mm to 2.62 mm. That is evidence about how the model was built — it was
fitted to the extractor's output in its own frame. So `--frame` has no default.

**Inferring intent from arithmetic is not reading intent.** An early `anchorFor`
preferred `T(4.61)` whenever a value happened to land on a round number in the
drawing's frame. A round-trip control caught it converting the author's own
`-2.30` — a station they chose in the model's frame — into a drawing station
they never wrote. The rule now: preserve the author's spelling for anything
unchanged, and only choose a representation for genuinely new knots.

**A clamp is an extrapolation wearing a disguise.** Asked for stations outside
the reference, `resample` returned the endpoint value, and the knot fitter
turned that flat run into knots that looked exactly like measurements. The first
`propose` produced a tail roofline of 0.976 m from a drawing with nothing to say
about that station. Refusing is the only safe behaviour; clamping silently is
the same class of error as measuring the wrong quantity.

**An improvement smaller than the reference's uncertainty is not an
improvement.** The first working `propose` reported a 0.19 mm gain for
replacing 23 of 25 knots and would have been accepted by any test that only
asked "did the number go down".

## What a design panel found that I had not

Four independent designs for the write step were produced and judged on four
lenses. Three of the grafts changed the implementation.

**Gating on `git status` is wrong in both directions.** It blocks during
ordinary uncommitted work, which is most of the time, and it opens the gate in
exactly the window after someone saves and commits and before they type the next
character. During this build the model repository went clean, then dirty, then
clean again within minutes. The gate is content now: a hash of the whole file
and a separate hash of the target curve's own bytes. If the curve's bytes moved
since the measurement, there is no flag to override it — that curve is somebody
else's work in flight. If only the rest of the file moved, the measurement still
stands and the write is offered under an explicit rebase.

**Derive the curve-to-measurement map, do not declare it.** The plan was a
hand-written table saying which curve corresponds to which reference profile.
That table is wrong the moment the model is refactored and nothing announces it.
Instead: shift the curve a millimetre in a scratch copy, rebuild, and see which
stations respond. Those are the stations it has authority over, measured from
the model as it is now. It immediately paid: `HWB` moves eleven parts and
nothing at all in the side silhouette, because it is a half-width curve and a
side drawing has no opinion about width. The tool refuses to fit it, and says
why.

**Prove the anchors are load-bearing rather than asserting it.** The claim that
`S.NOSE` and `T(4.715)` must be preserved rests on their being derived. So
perturb the constant each one references and require it to move, and require
everything else not to. That is the experiment showing what flattening them to
literals would cost, run every time instead of written in a comment.

**A round-trip identity gate breaks on the first reformat.** The judge on
durability sank a design for exactly the requirement I had built: refusing to
proceed unless the file re-renders byte-identical. It is the right property to
measure and the wrong thing to gate on, because a file that has never been
formatted will be one day.

And the anchor control failed the first time it ran — 36 of 42 — because it
perturbed the nose and expected knots anchored to the TAIL and the axles to
follow. Fourth time on this project that a control caught a wrong expectation
rather than a wrong instrument, which is now less a coincidence than a
description of what controls are for.

## The write that had to be reverted

Asked to write ZROOF in the axle frame, the tool did, and it was wrong.

The measurement was right: mean deviation 7.10 mm to 5.46 mm, a genuine 1.64 mm
improvement against the drawing. The result was a **263 mm notch cut into the
rear roofline** — 1.245 down to 0.982 and back to 1.250 across forty
millimetres.

The cause was a boundary. The knot at `T(4.70)` sits just outside the fitting
window and was correctly preserved, including the value a human had hand-tuned
an hour earlier for the D-pillar corner. The first *fitted* knot sits eleven
millimetres away, just inside the window, and took its value from the drawing's
tail extremity — where the outline has dropped to the bumper. Two knots eleven
millimetres apart, describing different features, 269 mm apart in value.

**A mean taken over four metres cannot see a notch forty millimetres wide.** The
acceptance rule was distance-only, so it accepted. Every guard the tool had
worked exactly as designed and none of them was looking at shape.

So the rule has a shape term now: a fit may move the curve, but it may not add
direction changes the curve did not have, and it may not be more than 1.6x
steeper than the original at its worst. Run against the same fit it reports
9.89x steeper, 29984 mm/m where the original's worst was 3032, and one added
direction change at exactly the notch. It refuses.

The general lesson, and it is the sharpest one on this project: **an acceptance
rule made of one number will be satisfied by something that ruins the other
numbers.** Finding 12 says a measurement is only as good as the argument that
the quantity moves. This is its complement — a measurement is only as good as
the argument that *nothing else* moved.

## The boundary check

The notch came from a fitting window whose edge fell where the drawing had
stopped describing the curve. Three things fix it, and each was found by the
previous one not being enough.

**Trim to where they agree.** The tell is available before any fitting: not the
size of the disagreement — a curve being refitted is *expected* to disagree —
but that the disagreement at the edge is orders of magnitude larger than
everywhere else. Across this roof the median gap is 0.77 mm; at the tail edge it
is 272 mm. Walk inward while the gap is a wild multiple of the median. That
alone took the artifact from a 263 mm cliff to a 6 mm dip.

**Then snap outward to whole spans.** The trimmed edge lands in the middle of
one of the author's own spans, leaving 45 mm between the last preserved knot and
the first valid reference sample across which nothing describes the curve. The
fit eases through that dead zone and dips 6 mm doing it. Snapping the window out
to the nearest authored knots means the fit replaces whole spans and every join
is at a knot somebody placed.

**And count reversals by depth, not by existence.** The first shape check used a
0.4 mm deadband and reported an added turning point on a roof that is flat to
within a millimetre over two metres — it was measuring sub-millimetre ripple. A
reversal now has to come back by 2 mm on both sides to count, the same idea as
topographic prominence. The 263 mm notch still fails it; a 0.3 mm wobble does
not.

With all three, the same fit that produced the notch now reports 1 turning point
before and after, steepest slope 3032 to 3035 mm/m — a ratio of 1.00 — and
improves the model by 1.16 mm. It is recommended rather than refused, and the
difference between those two outcomes is entirely in where the window ends.

## What it does not do yet

- **Nearest-surface distance is sampled**, so it is an upper bound that tightens
  with sample density. It is reported with its sample count and alongside the
  exact axis gaps, which need no sampling. A real triangle-to-triangle minimum
  with a BVH is the obvious next step.
- **The engine does not read images itself.** It consumes `measure_ortho.py`'s
  calibrated output. That is deliberate — see above — but it means a new
  drawing needs Python with numpy, scipy and PIL before the engine can use it.
- **Only the top profile is fitted.** `propose` refuses a curve that drives the
  lower outline, which is correct but leaves `ZB` unfittable until the bottom
  path is written.
- **Anchor promotion stays human.** The engine never invents a `T()` or an
  `S.NOSE` for a new knot — manufacturing one would fabricate a claim about what
  the manufacturer's drawing dimensions, which is a different kind of statement
  from "the curve passes through here". It preserves anchors forever once a
  human writes them.
- **No curve layer ownership.** Finding 09's kernel puts curves first, and a measurement
  correction should only ever edit a knot list. This engine now *produces* knot
  lists in the model's own form, but it does not own the curves; pasting them
  in is still a human step. Closing that — the engine writing the curve layer
  and the model rebuilding from it — is the next thing.
- **Only the side and front elevations.** A plan view would pin the half-widths
  along the length, which is currently the least-constrained part of the fit.
